5 A Uniform Factor-two Adaptivity Gap In this section, we prove our main result: for every pooled-testing instance and every test budget, the optimal dynamic welfare is at most twice the optimal static overlapping welfare. Equivalently, $\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{dyn}}(B,J)\leq 2\operatorname{OPT}^{*}(B,J).$ Fix a deterministic dynamic policy $\pi$ . It is enough to construct a distribution over feasible static allocations whose expected welfare is at least $V(\pi)/2$ , since the best deterministic static allocation must be at least as valuable as the average allocation in that distribution. A tempting construction is to sample a virtual infection profile, run $\pi$ on that profile, and use the pools encountered along the resulting path as a static allocation. This produces a feasible random static allocation, which can then be evaluated on an independent infection profile. The difficulty is that the pools on a dynamic path are selected in response to the outcomes generated by the virtual profile. Their selection is therefore correlated with the very information that makes them valuable dynamically. Once the recorded pools are evaluated on a fresh profile, this correlation disappears: an infected companion can prevent an entire pool from clearing, and the unmodified path allocation need not retain any constant fraction of the dynamic policy’s value. The solution is to half-thin the sampled path allocation: independently for every pool on the path, retain each agent in that pool with probability $1/2$ . Suppose we focus on whether a given agent (a focal agent) is cleared in this process. Thinning may discard this focal agent whose clearance we wish to preserve, but it may also discard infected companions who would otherwise prevent the focal agent from being cleared. The proof shows that these competing effects balance in aggregate. For each focal agent, a Boolean-tree charging argument compares the profiles on which the dynamic policy clears that agent with the probability that one of the corresponding thinned pools clears it on a complementary profile. The resulting loss is at most a factor of two. We first establish this comparison on a uniform Boolean subcube, where free infection coordinates can be paired with their complements. The core of this argument reduces the portion of the policy relevant to one focal agent to a query–offer model, defined formally below: tests that exclude the focal agent act as queries that determine the path, whereas tests that include it act as offers that may clear it. Our key combinatorial lemma analyzes this reduced tree. Finally, a two-profile coupling conditions arbitrary heterogeneous product priors into uniform Boolean subcubes. Averaging the resulting per-agent inequalities shows that the half-thinned path allocation has expected welfare at least $V(\pi)/2$ , completing the comparison with $\operatorname{OPT}^{*}(B,J)$ . Proof strategy. The proof compares the dynamic policy and the random static allocation one agent at a time by fixing a focal agent $i$ . We use one infection profile to select a path through the dynamic policy, half-thin the pools on that path, and evaluate the resulting static allocation on an independent infection profile. The central step is to prove that the probability this static allocation clears $i$ is at least half the probability that the original dynamic policy clears $i$ . Once this inequality is established for every agent, we multiply by the utilities and sum. Every realization of the half-thinned path is a feasible static allocation, so its expected welfare is bounded by $\operatorname{OPT}^{*}(B,J)$ , yielding the factor of two. Most of the work lies in proving the per-agent comparison. We begin on a uniform Boolean subcube, where infection profiles can be paired by flipping their free coordinates. From the perspective of a focal agent, $i$ , a test that excludes $i$ only helps determine which part of the policy is reached, while a test containing $i$ provides an opportunity to clear it. The query–offer tree introduced below formalizes this distinction and supports the required combinatorial argument. A two-profile coupling then reduces arbitrary heterogeneous product priors to these uniform-subcube comparisons. The following subsections develop each of these steps formally. Notation and half-thinning. Throughout this section, it is more convenient to use infection
indicators $Z_{i}:=1-X_{i},\qquad Z_{i}=1\text{ meaning infected}.$ For a deterministic policy $\pi$ , write $P_{\pi}(z)$ for the list of at most $B$ pools encountered when the infection profile is $z$ . Given any list of pools $P$ , its half-thinning independently retains each agent–pool incidence with probability $1/2$ , using coins independent of all infection profiles. Thus, if an agent appears in several pools, its retention is decided independently in each occurrence. Empty resulting pools are discarded. Because thinning can only decrease pool sizes, every half-thinned list is a feasible static overlapping allocation using at most $B$ tests. 5.1 Reduced Normalized Tree This section isolates the main combinatorial object at the heart of the proof. Let $E\subseteq[n]$ be a finite set of free Boolean coordinates, order $\{0,1\}^{E}$ coordinatewise, and for $z\in\{0,1\}^{E}$ , denote the support of an infection profile by $\operatorname{supp}(z):=\{j\in E:z_{j}=1\}$ . In addition we let $\bar{z}\in\{0,1\}^{E}$ be the companion profile to $z$ such that $\bar{z}_{i}=1-z_{i}$ . The object below is a bookkeeping device for comparing the two clearance events at the center of the proof. Fix a focal agent assumed to be healthy and imagine restricting the infection profiles to a Boolean subcube, so that the coordinates outside $E$ are already fixed. After the effects of those fixed coordinates are removed, a profile $z\in\{0,1\}^{E}$ determines the path followed through the dynamic policy (i.e. tests made). At the same time, every opportunity to clear the healthy focal agent is equipped with an independent thinning trial that records the probability that the corresponding half-thinned pool clears the focal agent when evaluated on the companion profile $\bar{z}$ . Roughly, tests that exclude the focal agent become queries , because they only determine which path is followed, while tests containing the focal agent become offers , because they provide opportunities to clear it. Thus, the same tree tracks both dynamic clearance under the path-selecting profile $(z)$ and randomized static clearance under its companion profile $(\bar{z})$ . Definition 5.1 (Reduced normalized tree) . Fix a finite set $E$ of free nonfocal coordinates, together with an implicit distinguished healthy focal agent not represented in $E$ . A reduced normalized tree on $E$ is a finite rooted tree, denoted by $T$ , whose leaves are unlabeled and whose internal nodes are of the following two
types: (i) A query node (test without focal agent) is labelled by a set $A\subseteq E$ and has two children, called its zero child and its positive child. For a profile $z\in\{0,1\}^{E}$ , the execution takes the zero child when $z_{A}=\mathbf{0},$ meaning that $z_{j}=0$ for every $j\in A$ , and takes the positive child otherwise. The tree is normalized in the sense that every query or offer label appearing in the subtree below the zero child is disjoint from $A$ . (ii) An offer node (test with focal agent) is labelled by a set $S\subseteq E$ and has one continuation child. When the execution reaches this node, an independent thinning trial is performed. Conditional on the profile $z$ , this trial succeeds with probability $p_{S}(z):=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1+|S\cap\operatorname{supp}(\bar{z})|}=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1+|S\setminus\operatorname{supp}(z)|}.$ (12) The thinning trial does not affect the path through the tree. If $z_{S}=\mathbf{0}$ , the dynamic execution stops at this offer and records a clearance of the focal agent under the dynamic execution. Otherwise, the execution proceeds to the unique continuation child. If the execution reaches a leaf, it terminates without recording a dynamic clearance of the focal agent and without performing any further thinning trials. All thinning trials encountered during an execution are mutually independent. We use the conventions $z_{\varnothing}=\mathbf{0}$ and $\operatorname{supp}(z)\cap\varnothing=\varnothing$ ; consequently, an offer with $S=\varnothing$ always stops the dynamic execution and has thinning-success probability $p_{\varnothing}(z)=1/2$ . The probability in equation 12 has a direct interpretation. For the thinned offer to clear the healthy focal agent under the companion profile, $\bar{z}$ , the thinning must retain the focal agent and discard every $j\in S$ for which $\bar{z}_{j}=1$ ( $z_{j}=0$ ). By contrast, every coordinate with $z_{j}=1$ becomes healthy under the companion profile, so its thinning outcome is irrelevant to clearance under the thinned offer. The focal agent contributes one required coin flip, and the $|S\cap\operatorname{supp}(\bar{z})|=|S\setminus\operatorname{supp}(z)|$ zero-coordinates of $z$ contribute one each. Fix a reduced normalized tree $T$ . For a profile $z$ , let $I_{T}(z)\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether the dynamic execution terminates at a stopping offer rather than at a leaf. Let $\mathcal{O}_{T}(z)$ be the collection of offer nodes encountered during the execution, including the stopping offer when one is reached. If $S_{v}$ denotes the label of offer node $v$ , define $F_{T}(z):=\prod_{v\in\mathcal{O}_{T}(z)}\bigl(1-p_{S_{v}}(z)\bigr),$ (13) where we let the empty product be one. Because the thinning trials are independent, $F_{T}(z)$ is the probability that every encountered offer fails under the companion profile $\bar{z}$ . Consequently, $1-F_{T}(z)$ is the probability that at least one thinning trial succeeds before the execution reaches a leaf or stops at a clearing offer. When the tree $T$ is fixed, we suppress the subscript and write $I(z)$ and $F(z)$ . A fully worked three-coordinate example, including the execution path and the values of $I(z)$ and $F(z)$ for every profile, is given in appendix B . 5.2 The Boolean-tree Charging Lemma With the definition of Reduced Normalized Trees in hand, we prove the key combinatorial lemma at the heart of our proof. In what follows, we say that a set $R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{E}$ is increasing if for $z\in R$ whenever $z^{\prime}\geq z$ (i.e. $z^{\prime}_{i}\geq z_{i}$ for all $i\in E$ ), then $z^{\prime}\in R$ . Similarly, $f:R\rightarrow[0,1]$ is decreasing if $z^{\prime}\geq z$ implies $f(z^{\prime})\leq f(z)$ . Lemma 5.2 (Monotone failure-weight invariant) . Let $R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{E}$ be increasing and let $f:R\to[0,1]$ be decreasing. Put $M:=\max_{z\in R}f(z)$ , with $M:=0$ if $R$ is empty. For every reduced normalized tree $T$ , $\sum_{z\in R}\left[f(z)F_{T}(z)(1+I_{T}(z))-1\right]\leq-|R|(1-M).$ (14) In particular, on the full cube, $\bigl|\{z:I_{T}(z)=1\}\bigr|\leq 2\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{E}}\bigl(1-F_{T}(z)\bigr).$ (15) Proof. For a current tree $T$ , write $W_{T}(R,f):=\sum_{z\in R}\left[f(z)F_{T}(z)(1+I_{T}(z))-1\right]$ for the left side of equation 14 , and below we abbreviate it to $W$ . We prove the bound by induction on the remaining tree depth. Base Case: The base case is a tree consisting of a single leaf in which case $I_{T}=0$ and $F_{T}=1$ , so $\sum_{z\in R}(f(z)-1)\leq|R|(M-1).$ Query Root: Let us now suppose first that the root is a query $A$ . Partition $R_{0}:=\{z\in R:z_{A}=\mathbf{0}\},\qquad R_{+}:=R\setminus R_{0}.$ Since $R$ is increasing, the set $R_{+}$ is also increasing. After fixing and deleting the coordinates $A$ , the set $R_{0}$ is increasing on the residual cube $\{0,1\}^{E\setminus A}$ : its projection contains the remaining-coordinate vectors of profiles in $R_{0}$ . The zero-child normalization makes its continuation independent of the deleted coordinates. In addition, the restrictions (and, on $R_{0}$ , projection) of $f$ remain decreasing. If their maxima are $M_{0}$ and $M_{+}$ , we use the fact that a query adds no thinning factor of its own and the induction hypothesis for the two children to prove the desired inequality: $W_{T}\leq-|R_{0}|(1-M_{0})-|R_{+}|(1-M_{+})\leq-|R|(1-M),$ Offer Root: Now suppose that the root is an offer $S$ . Write $k:=|S|$ and set $R_{\mathrm{z}}:=\{z\in R:z_{S}=\mathbf{0}\},\qquad R_{\mathrm{p}}:=R\setminus R_{\mathrm{z}},\qquad p_{0}:=2^{-k-1}.$ Profiles in $R_{\mathrm{z}}$ stop at this offer. On $R_{\mathrm{p}}$ define $f^{\prime}(z):=f(z)(1-p_{S}(z)).$ Let us start by considering the case where $R_{\mathrm{p}}$ is empty. One way this occurs is when $R$ is also empty. However, in this case, the lemma’s desired inequality holds from the fact that $W_{T}(R,f)=0$ (as the sum is empty), and $|R|=0$ . If $R_{\mathrm{p}}$ is empty and $R$ is non-empty, then it must be the case that $S=\varnothing$ . To see this, assume the contrary where $S\neq\varnothing$ and consider any $z\in R_{\mathrm{z}}$ . Notice that flipping one coordinate of (the non-empty) $S$ (to 1) would produce a profile in $R$ (since the set is increasing), which in turn would necessarily be in $R_{\mathrm{p}}$ , implying $R_{\mathrm{p}}$ is in fact non-empty, a contradiction. Thus let us continue with the case where $S=\varnothing$ . When $S=\varnothing$ , every $z\in R$ must accept at the root offer (so $I_{T}(z)=1$ for all $z\in R$ ), and in addition the thinning clearance probability at this offer is given by $p_{0}=1/2$ . As such we obtain $W_{T}(R,f)=\sum_{z\in R}f(z)(1-p_{0})(2)-1\leq|R|(M-1),$ which is the desired inequality of the lemma. Either way, we have shown the lemma holds when $R_{\mathrm{p}}$ is empty. Now let us continue to the case where $R_{\mathrm{p}}$ is nonempty. Once more, since $R$ is increasing, the set $R_{\mathrm{p}}$ is increasing. In addition, as coordinates change from zero to one, fewer thinning coins are forced to discard companions, so $p_{S}$ is increasing and $1-p_{S}$ is decreasing. Hence $f^{\prime}=f\cdot(1-p_{S})$ is decreasing. Moreover, every $z\in R_{\mathrm{p}}$ has at least one one-coordinate in $S$ , so $p_{S}(z)\geq 2^{-k}$ . In conjunction with the bound $f(z)\leq M$ , we get $p_{S}(z)\geq 2^{-k},\qquad M^{\prime}:=\max_{R_{\mathrm{p}}}f^{\prime}\leq M(1-2^{-k}).$ (16) To continue, partition the sum defining $W_{T}(R,f)$ according to $R=R_{\mathrm{z}}\sqcup R_{\mathrm{p}}$ : $\displaystyle W_{T}(R,f)$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in R}\left[f(z)F_{T}(z)\bigl(1+I_{T}(z)\bigr)-1\right]$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in R_{\mathrm{z}}}\left[f(z)F_{T}(z)\bigl(1+I_{T}(z)\bigr)-1\right]$ $\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{z\in R_{\mathrm{p}}}\left[f(z)F_{T}(z)\bigl(1+I_{T}(z)\bigr)-1\right].$ For the first sum, all $z\in R_{\mathrm{z}}$ clear the root offer, hence $I_{T}(z)=1$ . In addition, there is one thinning trial, which fails with probability $F(z)=1-p_{0}$ . It follows that the first sum is bounded by $|R_{\mathrm{z}}|\bigl[2M(1-p_{0})-1\bigr]$ . For continuing profiles, $z\in R_{\mathrm{p}}$ , suppose that $T_{\mathrm{p}}$ is the tree of the continuation child. Given that an offer is made at the root, we have $F_{T}(z)=(1-p_{S}(z))F_{T_{\mathrm{p}}}(z)$ , hence the second sum is: $\displaystyle\sum_{z\in R_{\mathrm{p}}}\left[f(z)F_{T}(z)\bigl(1+I_{T}(z)\bigr)-1\right]$ $\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{z\in R_{\mathrm{p}}}\left[f(z)\bigl(1-p_{S}(z)\bigr)F_{T_{\mathrm{p}}}(z)\bigl(1+I_{T_{\mathrm{p}}}(z)\bigr)-1\right]$ $\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{z\in R_{\mathrm{p}}}\left[f^{\prime}(z)F_{T_{\mathrm{p}}}(z)\bigl(1+I_{T_{\mathrm{p}}}(z)\bigr)-1\right]$ $\displaystyle\qquad=W_{T_{\mathrm{p}}}(R_{\mathrm{p}},f^{\prime}).$ Thus a continuing profile on the other hand is exactly the child expression with weight $f^{\prime}=f\cdot(1-p_{S})$ , which means we can directly apply the inductive hypothesis to bound it by $-|R_{\mathrm{p}}|(1-M^{\prime})$ . Putting everything together, we have the following bound: $W_{T}\leq|R_{\mathrm{z}}|\bigl[2M(1-p_{0})-1\bigr]-|R_{\mathrm{p}}|(1-M^{\prime}).$ (17) For every $z\in R_{\mathrm{z}}$ , hold its coordinates in $E\setminus S$ fixed and replace its all-zero restriction on $S$ by any of the $2^{k}-1$ nonzero patterns. Upward closure places every such extension in $R_{\mathrm{p}}$ . Extensions generated from distinct $z\in R_{\mathrm{z}}$ have different restrictions to $E\setminus S$ , so the families are disjoint. Therefore $|R_{\mathrm{p}}|\geq(2^{k}-1)|R_{\mathrm{z}}|.$ (18) Subtracting the target $-|R|(1-M)$ from the right side of equation 17 , and using equation 16 , along with the fact that $|R|=|R_{\mathrm{z}}|+|R_{\mathrm{p}}|$ , gives at most $\displaystyle M|R_{\mathrm{z}}|(1-2p_{0})-M|R_{\mathrm{p}}|2^{-k}$ $\displaystyle\leq M|R_{\mathrm{z}}|\left[(1-2^{-k})-(2^{k}-1)2^{-k}\right]$ $\displaystyle=0.$ This proves the invariant, including the case $k=0$ . All that remains is to obtain equation 15 . Take $R=\{0,1\}^{E}$ and $f\equiv 1$ in equation 14 . Its left side becomes $\sum_{z:I_{T}(z)=1}F(z)-\sum_{z}(1-F(z)),$ which the inequality proves is nonpositive. With $H(z):=1-F(z)$ , it follows that, $|\{z:I_{T}(z)=1\}|=\sum_{z:I_{T}(z)=1}(F(z)+H(z))\leq\sum_{z}H(z)+\sum_{z:I_{T}(z)=1}H(z)\leq 2\sum_{z}H(z).$ Here the first inequality uses $\sum_{z:I_{T}(z)=1}F(z)\leq\sum_{z}H(z)$ , which is exactly the aggregate invariant; the second uses $\{z:I_{T}(z)=1\}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{E}$ .
∎ Lemma intuition: failure-mass charging. The invariant we just proved (Equation 14 ) becomes more transparent after a simple rearrangement. Define $g(z):=f(z)F_{T}(z).$ Although the lemma permits an arbitrary decreasing function $f$ , in the induction one may regard $f(z)$ as the failure weight accumulated before entering the current subtree, as occurs in the offer-root induction. Since $F_{T}(z)$ is the probability that all thinning trials encountered within the subtree fail, $g(z)=f(z)F_{T}(z)$ is the total failure weight associated with profile $z$ , combining the failure weight accumulated before the subtree with the failure probability contributed by the subtree itself. Adding $|R|$ to both sides of the invariant and separating the profiles for which $I_{T}(z)=1$ gives the equivalent inequality $\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in R\\
I_{T}(z)=1\end{subarray}}f(z)F_{T}(z)\leq\sum_{z\in R}\bigl(M-f(z)F_{T}(z)\bigr).$ (19) This expression provides a failure-mass charging interpretation of the lemma. Imagine that each profile begins with a budget of $M$ units. Every profile retains $f(z)F_{T}(z)$ units of failure mass, while a profile at which the dynamic execution clears the focal agent requires an additional copy of this mass. The right-hand side of equation 19 is the total unused budget across all profiles, and the lemma says that this unused budget is large enough to pay for all of the additional failure mass carried by the dynamically stopping profiles.
Moreover, the charging is aggregate over $R$ : we compare the total available and required mass rather than comparing the two summands profile by profile. 5.3 From a Policy Tree to a Boolean Subcube A Boolean subcube of the full infection cube $\{0,1\}^{[n]}$ is specified by a set of free coordinates $E\subseteq[n]$ and a fixed assignment $a\in\{0,1\}^{[n]\setminus E}$ . The corresponding subcube is $C=C(a,E):=\bigl\{x\in\{0,1\}^{[n]}:x_{[n]\setminus E}=a\bigr\}.$ Thus, the coordinates in $E$ range freely over $\{0,1\}^{E}$ , while the coordinates in $[n]\setminus E$ remain fixed at $a$ . For each free-coordinate assignment $z\in\{0,1\}^{E}$ , let $x^{a}(z)\in C$ denote its unique extension to a full infection profile: $x^{a}(z)_{j}:=\begin{cases}z_{j},&j\in E,\\
a_{j},&j\notin E.\end{cases}$ If $\bar{z}\in\{0,1\}^{E}$ is the coordinatewise complement of $z$ , then the companion profile to $x^{a}(z)$ within $C$ is $\overline{x^{a}(z)}^{\,C}:=x^{a}(\bar{z}).$ In other words, the companion operation flips precisely the free coordinates and leaves the fixed coordinates unchanged. For the policy $\pi$ under consideration and a focal agent $i$ , define, for every full infection profile $x\in\{0,1\}^{[n]}$ , $d_{i}(x):=\mathbf{1}\bigl\{\pi\text{ dynamically clears }i\text{ under profile }x\bigr\}.$ For any list of pools $P$ and any full infection profile $y\in\{0,1\}^{[n]}$ , let $h_{i}(P,y)$ denote the probability, over the half-thinning coins, that the half-thinned version of $P$ clears agent $i$ when evaluated on profile $y$ . In particular, $h_{i}\bigl(P_{\pi}(x^{a}(z)),x^{a}(\bar{z})\bigr)$ is the probability that the half-thinned path allocation selected under $x^{a}(z)$ clears $i$ when evaluated on its companion profile $x^{a}(\bar{z})$ . Lemma 5.3 (Subcube half-thinning) . Let $\pi$ be a normalized deterministic policy, and let $C=C(a,E):=\bigl\{x\in\{0,1\}^{[n]}:x_{[n]\setminus E}=a\bigr\}$ be a Boolean subcube with free-coordinate set $E$ . For every focal agent $i$ , $\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{E}}d_{i}\bigl(x^{a}(z)\bigr)\leq 2\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{E}}h_{i}\bigl(P_{\pi}(x^{a}(z)),x^{a}(\bar{z})\bigr).$ (20) Proof. We consider the three possible statuses of the focal coordinate: it may be fixed infected, fixed healthy, or free on the subcube. Case 1: Focal Agent Fixed Infected. Suppose first that $i\notin E$ and $a_{i}=1$ , so that $i$ is fixed infected throughout $C$ . An infected agent cannot be cleared by a negative test, and hence $d_{i}\bigl(x^{a}(z)\bigr)=0\qquad\text{for every }z\in\{0,1\}^{E}.$ The left-hand side of equation 20 is therefore zero,
so the inequality is immediate. Case 2: Focal Agent Fixed Healthy. Now suppose that $i\notin E$ and $a_{i}=0$ , so that $i$ is fixed healthy. We reduce the part of the policy tree relevant to the clearance of $i$ to a reduced normalized tree on the free coordinates $E$ . Along each policy path, we retain the execution only through the first negative pool containing $i$ , if one is reached. After that pool, agent $i$ has already been cleared, so later tests are irrelevant to its dynamic clearance. Consider first a tested pool not containing $i$ . Fixed healthy members may be deleted. If the pool contains a fixed infected member, its outcome is always positive, so the corresponding node may be contracted to its positive child. Otherwise, after deleting the fixed coordinates, the pool becomes a query labelled by its free members $A\subseteq E$ . Its outcome is negative exactly when $z_{A}=\mathbf{0}$ . Following such a negative outcome, every agent in $A$ has been cleared; normalization of $\pi$ therefore ensures that all later pool labels exclude $A$ . This is precisely the zero-child exclusion required of a reduced normalized
tree. Next consider a tested pool containing $i$ . Fixed healthy companions may again be deleted. If the pool contains a fixed infected companion, it is always positive dynamically, so we contract the node to its positive child and omit its thinning trial. This leaves the dynamic clearance indicator unchanged. It can only decrease the static clearance probability, since thinning might otherwise discard the fixed infected companion, and therefore makes the desired inequality
harder to prove. Every remaining pool containing $i$ becomes an offer labelled by its free companions $S\subseteq E$ . Since $i$ is fixed healthy, the offer clears $i$ dynamically exactly when $z_{S}=\mathbf{0}.$ Its paired evaluator is $x^{a}(\bar{z})$ . Under this profile, a free companion $j\in S$ is infected exactly when $z_{j}=0$ . Consequently, the half-thinned offer clears $i$ exactly when thinning retains $i$ and discards every member of $S$ whose coordinate in $z$ is zero. This occurs with probability $2^{-1-|S\setminus\operatorname{supp}(z)|},$ as required by equation 12 in the definition of a reduced normalized tree. Let $T$ be the resulting reduced normalized tree. By construction, $I_{T}(z)=d_{i}\bigl(x^{a}(z)\bigr),$ while $1-F_{T}(z)\leq h_{i}\bigl(P_{\pi}(x^{a}(z)),x^{a}(\bar{z})\bigr).$ Here the inequality arises because the reduction deletes some thinning opportunities and truncates the tree after the focal agent is dynamically cleared. Retaining those additional opportunities can only increase the static clearance probability. Applying equation 15 therefore gives $\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{E}}d_{i}\bigl(x^{a}(z)\bigr)$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{E}}I_{T}(z)$ $\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{E}}\bigl(1-F_{T}(z)\bigr)$ $\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{E}}h_{i}\bigl(P_{\pi}(x^{a}(z)),x^{a}(\bar{z})\bigr).$ This proves the lemma when the focal coordinate is fixed healthy. Case 3: Focal Agent Free Coordinate. It remains to consider the case $i\in E$ . Set $E^{\prime}:=E\setminus\{i\}.$ For every $v\in\{0,1\}^{E^{\prime}}$ , let $\bar{v}$ denote its coordinatewise complement in $\{0,1\}^{E^{\prime}}$ , and define two full profiles $x^{H}(v),x^{I}(v)\in C$ by $x^{H}(v)_{i}=0,\qquad x^{I}(v)_{i}=1,$ and $x^{H}(v)_{E^{\prime}}=x^{I}(v)_{E^{\prime}}=v,\qquad x^{H}(v)_{[n]\setminus E}=x^{I}(v)_{[n]\setminus E}=a.$ These profiles form the healthy and infected faces $C^{H}:=\{x^{H}(v):v\in\{0,1\}^{E^{\prime}}\},\qquad C^{I}:=\{x^{I}(v):v\in\{0,1\}^{E^{\prime}}\},$ and partition the original subcube: $C=C^{H}\sqcup C^{I}.$ The companion operation on $C$ exchanges the two faces: $\overline{x^{H}(v)}^{\,C}=x^{I}(\bar{v}),\qquad\overline{x^{I}(v)}^{\,C}=x^{H}(\bar{v}).$ Since the focal agent is infected under every profile in $C^{I}$ , $d_{i}(x^{I}(v))=0.$ Similarly, when the selector is $x^{H}(v)$ , its companion in the full subcube is $x^{I}(\bar{v})$ , under which the focal agent is infected. Therefore, $h_{i}\bigl(P_{\pi}(x^{H}(v)),x^{I}(\bar{v})\bigr)=0.$ Consequently, the two sides of equation 20 reduce respectively to $\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{E}}d_{i}\bigl(x^{a}(z)\bigr)$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{E^{\prime}}}d_{i}(x^{H}(v)),$ (21) $\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{E}}h_{i}\bigl(P_{\pi}(x^{a}(z)),x^{a}(\bar{z})\bigr)$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{E^{\prime}}}h_{i}\bigl(P_{\pi}(x^{I}(v)),x^{H}(\bar{v})\bigr).$ (22) Regard $C^{H}$ as a subcube with free-coordinate set $E^{\prime}$ and with the focal coordinate fixed healthy. The fixed-healthy case proved above then gives $\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{E^{\prime}}}d_{i}(x^{H}(v))\leq 2\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{E^{\prime}}}h_{i}\bigl(P_{\pi}(x^{H}(v)),x^{H}(\bar{v})\bigr).$ (23) We claim that, for every $v\in\{0,1\}^{E^{\prime}}$ , $h_{i}\bigl(P_{\pi}(x^{H}(v)),x^{H}(\bar{v})\bigr)\leq h_{i}\bigl(P_{\pi}(x^{I}(v)),x^{H}(\bar{v})\bigr).$ (24) To prove the claim, compare the executions of $\pi$ under $x^{H}(v)$ and $x^{I}(v)$ . These profiles differ only in the infection status of $i$ . Let $t$ be the first pool that clears $i$ in the execution under $x^{H}(v)$ , if such a pool exists. Before $t$ , a tested pool not containing $i$ has the same outcome under both profiles. A tested pool containing $i$ must be positive under $x^{H}(v)$ , since otherwise it would clear $i$ before $t$ ; it is also positive under $x^{I}(v)$ because $i$ is infected there. Inductively, the two executions therefore select exactly the same pools through and including $t$ . At $t$ , the outcome is negative under $x^{H}(v)$ and positive under $x^{I}(v)$ . Evaluate both selected path allocations on the same profile $x^{H}(\bar{v})$ , and couple their half-thinning coins on their common pool occurrences. Because $\pi$ is normalized, no pool selected after $t$ under $x^{H}(v)$ contains $i$ . Hence every thinning opportunity that can clear $i$ in $P_{\pi}(x^{H}(v))$ occurs in the common prefix through $t$ , which is also contained in $P_{\pi}(x^{I}(v))$ . Thus every thinning outcome that clears $i$ using the former path also clears it using the latter path. The infected execution may provide additional opportunities after $t$ , proving equation 24 . If no such pool $t$ exists, every pool containing $i$ tests positive under both profiles, while pools excluding $i$ again have identical outcomes. The two executions then follow the same path for their entire duration, and equation 24 holds with equality. Summing equation 24 over $v$ and combining it with equation 23 gives $\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{E^{\prime}}}d_{i}(x^{H}(v))\leq 2\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{E^{\prime}}}h_{i}\bigl(P_{\pi}(x^{I}(v)),x^{H}(\bar{v})\bigr).$ Together with equations 21 and 22 , this is exactly equation 20 .
∎ 5.4 Symmetrization for Arbitrary Product Priors The subcube lemma is an unweighted statement: it compares uniform sums over a Boolean subcube. We now show that two independent draws from an arbitrary heterogeneous product prior can be decomposed into a random subcube together with a uniform orientation of its free coordinates. This converts the subcube inequality into the desired product-distribution inequality without imposing any bounds on individual infection
probabilities. Theorem 5.4 (Per-agent half-thinning inequality) . Let $Z$ and $Z^{\prime}$ be independent infection profiles drawn from the instance’s product prior. For every normalized deterministic policy $\pi$ and every agent $i$ , $\mathbb{P}\bigl(\pi\text{ dynamically clears }i\bigr)\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[h_{i}\bigl(P_{\pi}(Z),Z^{\prime}\bigr)\right].$ (25) Proof. For each coordinate $j\in[n]$ , define the unordered pair $U_{j}:=\bigl(\min\{Z_{j},Z^{\prime}_{j}\},\max\{Z_{j},Z^{\prime}_{j}\}\bigr)\in\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,1)\},$ and let $U:=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n})$ . We condition on a realization of $U$ having positive probability. This realization determines a set of free coordinates $E:=\{j\in[n]:U_{j}=(0,1)\},$ namely the coordinates on which $Z$ and $Z^{\prime}$ disagree. On every coordinate $j\notin E$ , the two profiles agree, and we define their common fixed value by $a_{j}:=Z_{j}=Z^{\prime}_{j}.$ Thus $U$ determines the Boolean subcube $C(a,E)=\bigl\{x\in\{0,1\}^{[n]}:x_{[n]\setminus E}=a\bigr\}.$ It remains to determine the conditional distribution of the free coordinates. For every $j\in E$ , the two possible orientations have the same probability: $\mathbb{P}(Z_{j}=0,Z^{\prime}_{j}=1)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{j}=1,Z^{\prime}_{j}=0),$ because $Z_{j}$ and $Z^{\prime}_{j}$ are independent draws from the same marginal distribution. Consequently, $\mathbb{P}(Z_{j}=0\mid U_{j}=(0,1))=\mathbb{P}(Z_{j}=1\mid U_{j}=(0,1))=\frac{1}{2}.$ Moreover, the coordinate pairs $(Z_{j},Z^{\prime}_{j})$ are independent across $j$ . Conditioning separately on their unordered values therefore leaves these orientation choices independent across the coordinates
in $E$ . Let $V:=Z_{E}\in\{0,1\}^{E}.$ Conditional on $U$ , the vector $V$ is uniform on $\{0,1\}^{E}$ , and the two full profiles can be written as $Z=x^{a}(V),\qquad Z^{\prime}=x^{a}(\bar{V}).$ Thus, conditional on $U$ , the first profile is uniform on the subcube $C(a,E)$ and the second is exactly its companion within that subcube. Applying lemma 5.3 to $C(a,E)$ and dividing its two sums by $2^{|E|}$ gives $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[d_{i}(Z)\mid U\right]$ $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{|E|}}\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{E}}d_{i}\bigl(x^{a}(v)\bigr)$ $\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{2^{|E|}}\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{E}}h_{i}\bigl(P_{\pi}(x^{a}(v)),x^{a}(\bar{v})\bigr)$ $\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\left[h_{i}\bigl(P_{\pi}(Z),Z^{\prime}\bigr)\mathrel{\Big|}U\right].$ Finally, taking expectations over $U$ and applying the law of iterated
expectation yields $\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(\pi\text{ dynamically clears }i\bigr)$ $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[d_{i}(Z)]$ $\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[h_{i}\bigl(P_{\pi}(Z),Z^{\prime}\bigr)\right],$ which proves equation 25 .
∎ Theorem 5.5 (Uniform factor-two adaptivity gap) . For every population $J$ , test budget $B$ , and pool-size cap $G$ , $\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{dyn}}(B,J)\leq 2\operatorname{OPT}^{*}(B,J),\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad\mathcal{A}(B,J)\leq 2.$ (26) Proof. Fix an arbitrary deterministic policy $\pi$ . By lemma 3.5 , we may assume without loss of generality that $\pi$ is normalized. Sample the virtual profile $Z$ , follow $P_{\pi}(Z)$ , and half-thin every incidence. The resulting random list contains at most $B$ feasible pools and is sampled independently of the true evaluation profile $Z^{\prime}$ . It is therefore a randomized feasible static allocation. More explicitly, after fixing $Z$ and all thinning coins, one obtains a deterministic list of at most $B$ pools, each of size at most $G$ , whose expected welfare under $Z^{\prime}$ is at most $\operatorname{OPT}^{*}(B,J)$ . Averaging this pointwise bound over $Z$ and the thinning coins preserves the inequality. Multiply equation 25 by $u_{i}$ and sum over agents: $V(\pi)\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i}u_{i}h_{i}(P_{\pi}(Z),Z^{\prime})\right]\leq 2\operatorname{OPT}^{*}(B,J).$ Taking $\pi$ to be optimal proves the theorem.
